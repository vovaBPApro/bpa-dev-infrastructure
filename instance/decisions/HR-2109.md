## The design

Two bots, two chats, two privilege levels:

| | administrator | orchestrator |
|---|---|---|
| runs as | root | its own unprivileged user |
| chat | separate, operator-only | the development chat |
| takes commands from | the operator only | the operator |
| takes from the orchestrator | **requests only** — "ask him for X" | n/a |
| lifetime | always on; machine up ⇒ admin up | sessions may be raised and killed freely |
| purpose | authority, recovery, machine administration | development |

## Why it is the right shape — his argument, which is the strong part

> ти можеш бути заінтересованим в підробці, бо у тебе багато задач … якщо ти щось
> підробиш, то тобі роботи стане менше. Адміністратору пофігу

The point is not whether the orchestrator is honest. It is that **the construction must
not depend on the orchestrator's honesty**, because the orchestrator has something to gain
from forging an approval and the administrator has nothing. This is the same reasoning
that made the auto-park have no agent-callable reset — and this row supplies the operator
door that was missing (V3-0.29).

It also removes the SSH-key requirement, which failed his real constraint: he decides from
a phone, from anywhere, and must not need to be at a computer.

## The load-bearing condition — and the operator's correction to the orchestrator

**The orchestrator must not be able to *write* the authorization record.** The record is
written by a root-owned process; the orchestrator runs as `bpa` and may only read it.
That single fact is what makes the button mean something.

The orchestrator initially claimed a second condition of equal weight — that the
administrator's bot token must be unreadable by the orchestrator's user, "else I
impersonate the admin and send the button myself". **The operator rejected that argument
and was right** (Telegram 2111):

> Та навіть якщо так, навіть якщо надішлеш, то у тебе ж нема root-прав. Ти будеш
> запускатись під користувачем ppa, а адмін під root-ом. В цьому трюк.

Holding the token would not let the orchestrator write the record, because writing is a
privilege question, not a messaging question. What a leaked token would actually buy is
**deception and interception** — messaging the operator in the administrator's voice, or
consuming a callback so the administrator never sees the press. Both are real and worth
a root-owned token at mode 0600; neither is the wall. Recorded here in his framing rather
than the orchestrator's, because the distinction between "the boundary" and "hygiene
around the boundary" is exactly the kind of thing that gets mis-implemented later.

The remaining condition stands: **the administrator accepts requests from the
orchestrator, never commands.** A request is "ask the operator whether to unpark V3-1.9".
The administrator decides what to show, the operator decides the answer, and the
administrator alone writes the record.

## Consequences to carry

- This supersedes the SSH-signature approach for operator authority (V3-0.29 round 2's
  `gate/operator-unpark.sh`). The signature machinery may still be the *recording* format;
  what changes is who holds the signing authority — the administrator, on the operator's
  button, rather than the operator's laptop.
- It requires V3-1.9: the orchestrator and its lanes must run non-root, or "root-owned"
  means nothing.
- The administrator is a **must-have** service — machine up implies administrator up. The
  orchestrator's own session lifecycle stays as it is.
- It gives the operator a recovery path to a wedged orchestrator without touching the
  machine, which today does not exist.
- Not to be built on the v2 line; v3 only.
